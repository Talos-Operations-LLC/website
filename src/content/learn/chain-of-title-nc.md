## What is a chain of title?

Each deed is one link. It names who conveyed the property (the grantor) and who received it (the grantee). When the grantee on one deed is the grantor on the next, the links connect. The chain of title is the whole sequence, from an earlier deed to the current owner.

## How is the grantor/grantee index organized?

Each county's Register of Deeds indexes every recorded document under the names of its grantors and its grantees, with the recording date, the book and page, and the instrument type. [Grantor vs. grantee](/learn/grantor-vs-grantee/) walks through an index entry.

## How do you walk the chain forward from a known deed?

1. Start from a deed you know, using a book and page from the tax card or an earlier document.
2. Note the grantee name or names and the recording date.
3. Search the grantor index for each grantee name, from that recording date forward.
4. Open the next deed that conveys this parcel and confirm its property description matches.
5. Repeat with the new grantee until there is no later conveyance. That grantee is the current owner of record.

Walking backward works the same way through the grantee index.

## What does a gap or break look like?

- **Name variants.** The next deed is indexed under a different spelling, initial or name order, so the chain looks broken until the variant is searched.
- **Unrecorded transfers.** A deed that was signed but never recorded doesn't appear in the index, so the next recorded deed's grantor doesn't match the last grantee.
- **Estates.** When an owner dies, the property can pass under a will or by inheritance without a new deed, so the next deed's grantor may be an heir or an executor. In North Carolina, the Clerk of Superior Court in each county handles the administration of estates.
- **Trusts and entities.** Property moves into a trust or an entity, and the next link is indexed under the trustee's or the entity's name.

## When do people order title work instead of checking the index themselves?

A chain-of-title check through the index answers one question: who holds title now, and through which deeds. Title work done by an attorney or abstractor typically covers more than the deeds, such as deeds of trust and their cancellations, liens, judgments and other recorded matters. People generally order title work when that wider scope matters, such as for a sale or a refinance, and check the index themselves when the question is only who owns the property.
